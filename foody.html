The app

A bilingual meal-planning app that acts like a nutritionist and a chef working side by side. By inputting dietary preferences, cuisines, macro targets, and a per-portion budget, users get four complete recipes with full nutrition data, cost estimates, instructions, and a grocery list, with instant switching between English and Spanish. To ensure reliability without sacrificing creativity, I paired a rule-based recipe engine with an AI enhancement layer that gracefully degrades and explains why if an external call fails. During development, I maintained a 50-entry bug diary to systematically trace and eliminate the three root causes responsible for nearly all system bugs.

What it does

  • Personalized recipes: combines 411 ingredients across 8 food groups into dishes that respect 21 diets, from Vegan and Keto to Low-FODMAP and Mediterranean.
  • Nutrition you can trust: protein, carbs, fats and fiber, broken down into subtypes, plus 29 vitamins and minerals measured against daily recommended values.
  • Never over your limit: portions are scaled automatically so every recipe stays within your calorie target.
  • Budget-aware: an optional cost cap per portion, priced from U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics grocery data and converted across 5 currencies.
  • World cuisines on a map: pick several of 23 regional cuisines from a list or an interactive world map.
  • AI copilot: suggests swaps and add-ins that respect your diet. A vegetarian is never offered chicken, and it understands requests in both languages.
  • Grocery list and recipe library: save favorites, revisit history and send ingredients to a store-specific shopping list.

How I built it

  • Python + Flask backend: handles data processing with Pandas, the recipe rules engine, and AI integration with Gemini and OpenAI, falling back automatically if a key fails.
  • Vanilla JavaScript + Tailwind frontend: no heavy framework, so the app stays fast, portable and easy to deploy as a single page.
  • Data-driven design: ingredients, diets and nutrient references live in CSV files, so adding a food or a translation is a data change, not a code change.

What went wrong, and what it taught me

I kept a bug diary. It reached 50 entries, and when I grouped them, three causes explained almost all of them:

  1. The same information lived in more than one place. Translations were stored in three places; portions lived in both a dropdown and the recipe object; store settings and city data could drift apart. Fix: one source of truth for each.
  2. Silent failures. A wrong key, a category name that never matched, or a store missing from a config fell back quietly instead of failing. Fix: match real names, and make failures visible.
  3. Patch-style editing. Find-and-replace removed a closing tag or brace, and one early error cascaded into everything after it. Fix: replace whole blocks, fix the first error first, hard-refresh before judging.

Two examples

  • Spanish titles had the wrong word order. The title was built from a fixed English pattern. I redesigned it as “X con Y, estilo italiano”, so no gender agreement is needed.
  • Calories above the limit. Per-serving calories were divided by portions a second time, so the cap never triggered. The fix removed the extra division and scaled all nutrients together.

Decisions I would defend

Protein target wins over the calorie limit, with a visible conflict flag. Saved recipes are snapshots in the language they were saved in. “Tabs are for browsing, the Lab is for editing.” Prices are labeled estimates, and a fake cooking-time filter was removed instead of kept.

Known limits and next steps

The grocery list shows recipe quantities (“4 oz of tuna”) where people buy packages (“1 can”). The data model has no packaging layer; the plan is a package-size table with round-up. Next: link-based recipe sharing, then accounts and a database.

What this shows

Finding root causes instead of patching symptoms, designing for failure (the AI fallback), and keeping an honest record of mistakes.
