A sales forecast that tests itself before you trust it

The tool

Most small businesses have the data but not the analyst. I built this tool for my consultancy, Analytix Business Strategy, to give clients the analysis a large company would pay a team for, from a single CSV upload.

The tool forecasts sales by running a tournament of 12 models, each tested on months it had not seen. When scores tie, it picks the simplest model, and its forecast ranges come from the model's own past errors. The chosen model missed by 2.6% on average, against 3.5% for a “same as last year” guess.

Beyond the forecast, it covers products, competitor brands, pricing and discount sensitivity, profit leakage, customer retention and cohorts, channels, customer sentiment and advertising impact. Every chart has a written finding beside it: what it means, what to do, and how confident the tool is. The findings roll up into a SWOT, drafted by rules or by Gemini AI.

I designed it to be honest about its limits. It checks the data before analyzing it, it flags when source files do not reconcile, it switches to simpler models when history is short, and it labels associations as associations. The demo's ad spend is simulated, so it is kept out of the forecast models; letting it in would have let them “see the future”.

What I did

  • Forecasting: a 12-model backtest tournament, a simplicity rule for ties, ranges from real past errors, and what-if and goal planning.
  • Marketing analytics: the delay between ad spend and sales, profit-based incremental ROI, and diminishing returns.
  • Customer analytics: retention, churn, cohorts, and profit per acquisition dollar.
  • Commercial analytics: pricing and price sensitivity, profit leakage, brand comparison, and channels.
  • Product: a Flask web app deployed on Render, CSV upload with data-quality checks, PDF and CSV export, and an optional Gemini SWOT.

The bug that mattered

My first forecast missed by about 11%, worse than the naive guess. The large November and December peaks were tilting the trend line upward. I changed the model to estimate trend and seasonality together, repeated three times, and the miss dropped to about 2.3% on that version. The app now shows the baseline comparison on screen, so a visitor can see whether the model beats a simple guess.

Judgment calls

  • Revenue definition. Only completed orders count (cancelled, returned and pending excluded): $144.2M, not the $177M in the dataset's summary file.
  • Mismatched files. The item-level file did not add up to the orders file, so I split each order's revenue across its items.
  • Currencies. Seven currencies on the same scale, treated as reported; the README says so.
  • Rising and declining products. Swings like +250% came from tiny bases, so the list covers only products of at least median size.
  • Honest limits. Price sensitivity is an association, and flat 60% yearly repurchase suggests the sample data is generated. The page says so.

Limits and next steps

The models are written in plain NumPy because statsmodels and Prophet were not available, so “Auto-SARIMA” is SARIMA-style. The ad-spend data in the sample is simulated and labeled. Next: column mapping for uploads and saved views.
